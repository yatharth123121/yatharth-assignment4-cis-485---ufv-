Executive Summary

The Fraser Valley was particularly affected by COVID-19 and the early warning systems were not effective (Office of the Auditor General of Canada [OAG], 2021). This is an idea to pose the question: how will we spend $1,000,000,000 so that the region does not have to choose between losing lives and closing the economy? Our response is to create the Fraser Valley Pandemic Operations Centre (FV-POC) which is a permanent regional department hosted by Fraser Health and located in Abbotsford, with capacity and supply locations in Chilliwack and Mission, that can detect the threat early, holds the supplies and capacity to respond locally, protects those who were most impacted, and communicates in a language and format that is trusted by each community.

The project will begin a pre-initiation phase in December 2026 and formally close the project in December 31, 2029. The funding is divided among 10 areas; facilities get 180 million dollars, surveillance and data get 120 million dollars, supplies and logistics get 150 million dollars surge capacity gets 140 million dollars, workforce and training get 90 million dollars communications get 40 million dollars an equity and community partnership fund gets 50 million dollars, a project management and quality fund gets 50 million dollars a contingency reserve gets 70 million dollars and a sustainment reserve gets 110 million dollars to support the centre after the project ends. Each group is broken down to the dollar in Section 3.

Economic and humanitarian central argument. Canada’s economy shrank by around five per cent in 2020 – its worst year ever as recorded by the National Statistic Agency (Statistics Canada, 2021), and economists estimate that the annual losses from future pandemics in the world will be hundreds of billions of dollars (Fan et al., 2018; Glennerster et al., 2023). The lowest cost way to preventing a shutdown during a pandemic is to spend money beforehand.

A. The Fraser Valley has a need for this project.

A1. The Early Warning System in Canada failed

In an OAG report (2021), the Public Health Agency of Canada was found to have failed to perform as its two primary early-warning tools, the Global Public Health Intelligence Network and its risk-assessment process were supposed to. A new virus was reported for the first time though without an alert being sent, however, a daily routine report was sent to subscribers (OAG, 2021). The shortcomings – such as outdated information technology and data sharing to provinces – identified by previous auditors and never remedied, were highlighted in media coverage of the audit (CBC News, 2021). The lesson to be learned from a regional project is: Do not expect a national system to blow the whistle.

A2. The Fraser Valley was most affected

The Fraser Valley was experiencing the bulk of the province’s COVID-19 cases on one day, with the Fraser Health region reporting about 74 per cent of the cases, and the Provincial Health Officer making a public statement that the Fraser Valley was being hit harder than other communities (Business in Vancouver, 2020). Abbotsford was experiencing 7,607 cases and 8 per cent of BC’s cases for the 3 per cent of the population in April 2021 as reported in the Abbotsford News (2021). Occasionally outbreaks were reported within workplaces and hospitals. One Abbotsford blueberry-packing facility was found to be infected in July 2020 (Richmond News, 2020) and another in March 2021 at Chilliwack General Hospital and Abbotsford Regional Hospital (Global News, 2021). The three groups around which our project is built are shown in these examples, essential agricultural workers, hospital patients and staff, and seniors in congregate care.

A3. The most expensive were the residents of care homes.

The Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI, 2021) found that three per cent of Canadians who tested positive for COVID-19 lived in long-term care homes and 43 per cent of their deaths occurred there. Between March 2020 and August 2021, more than 56,000 residents of LTC/retirement home facilities and almost 22,000 staff at these facilities were infected and more than 14,000 residents and staff died (CIHI, 2021). A regional agency that can monitor care homes, supply them with protective equipment and help fix ventilation problems would fill this gap.

A4. This shutdown was far more expensive than any of the alternatives that we propose.

Real GDP in Canada fell by 5.3 per cent in 2020, with British Columbia contributing 0.51 percentage points of that decline (Statistics Canada, 2021). The Global Preparedness Monitoring Board (2020) described the world’s response as a collective failure to make preparedness a priority and said the return on preparedness spending is very large compared with the cost of a pandemic. According to Fan et al. (2018), the estimated cost of expected losses to the world due to pandemic risk is around 0.6 per cent of world income ($500 billion). Similarly, Glennerster et al. (2023) calculated that a number of hundreds of billions of dollars would likely be the net gains of any preparedness advance investment. The $1 billion project is small compared with what the loss of another shutdown would be.

A5. Technically proven as being the key to early detection.

Wastewater monitoring provides up to days ahead of warning. In Ottawa, there was an observation of an increase in viral RNA in wastewater about 48 hours prior to a corresponding increase in the virus in clinical samples and 96 hours before hospitalizations were observed (D’Aoust et al., 2020). At the University of Saskatchewan in Saskatoon researchers found that changes in wastewater happened one week before changes in case numbers at testing centres (University of Saskatchewan, 2020). In a study a Toronto shelter tested wastewater and found signs of COVID before it was detected in patients, which helped with early testing in a tough situation (“Early Identification,” 2023). These findings back up the surveillance system that’s, at the heart of FV-POC.

A6. Overlapping emergency situation in the region.

An atmospheric river storm hit Sumas Prairie in November, 2021, causing some 1,100 homes to be evacuated and isolating Chilliwack and Hope from the rest of the country by road (UFV Library, n.d.). Insured losses alone were over $675 million (Canadian Underwriter, 2025). Evacuation orders were also re-issued in Abbotsford and the Fraser Valley Regional District in December 2025 (Global News, 2025). The potential for a pandemic during a flood means that FV-POC buildings, supplies and communications need to be strategically placed and designed to remain functional during other emergencies.

2. Scope Page

The project’s scope is what it will and will not bring (Schwalbe, 2019). FV-POC’s scope statement is: to design, build, staff, test and deliver a Fraser Health regional pandemic operations centre with early detection, supply and surge capacity, protection of high-risk populations, and trusted communication to the Fraser Health communities of Abbotsford, Chilliwack, Mission, Hope, Agassiz and nearby rural areas.

In scope

  • One operations centre headquarters in Abbotsford and two satellite operations centres (Chilliwack and Mission).
  • A regional wastewater and sentinel-site surveillance network, including a public-health laboratory.
  • A data platform that is protected by privacy and has a dashboard.
  • A stockpile of equipment, tests and consumables, rotated and stored in a warehouse.
  • Modular surge-bed kits, ventilation retrofits for hospitals and care homes, and mobile clinics.
  • A permanent staff, a trained reserve corps and a UFV readiness academy.
  • Multilingual and culturally appropriate communication and outreach.
  • Grants to First Nations, municipalities, care homes and farmworker employers (the equity and community fund).
  • Three full-scale exercises and independent assessment.

Out of scope

  • Construction of a general hospital (the project does not build a new general hospital, but rather reinforces existing facilities).
  • Day-to-day clinical care, which continues to stay with Fraser Health.
  • Federal powers will be retained for border control and federal quarantine.
  • Vaccine research and development; FV-POC will only store and distribute vaccines.

The list will be amended as necessary and subject to the change-control procedure outlined in Section 15.

5. The Three Spheres of Systems Management

There are three spheres of project management, according to Schwalbe (2019), the business, the organization and the technology. When the three spheres are all considered, a project is likely to be successful and create something that will benefit the people that will use it.

I think the question is whether or not the project is worth it, and who benefits from the spending of that money. The intention is to avoid social losses that are greater than the cost as shown in Section 14. The primary business risk is that the value is not apparent in good years and the project must demonstrate clearly that there are indicators such as detection lead time and stock preparedness.

I know that the question is who’s controlling the organization. FV-POC is provided by Fraser Health, but is not a replacement for Fraser Health, the municipalities or the province. It should be a coordinating mechanism and should report to the regional champion as well as to a steering committee consisting of the federal funder, the province, the municipalities, First Nations and UFV. There needs to be an understanding of who makes the decisions, who pays and who speaks. Data‑sharing agreements are signed in the first six months, as there are issues identified in the OAG (2021) with sharing data between levels of government.

I think the question I want to ask is what tools do you need, the technology. They are comprised of wastewater testing, lab sequencing, data platform, inventory system, and a multilingual alert service. We need to have demonstrated technologies and not experimental technologies, so that the project can be completed by 2029.

6. The Four Frames of Organizations: Where Does UFV Fit?

Bolman and Deal (2017) suggest that the following four frames are used to understand an organization: structural, human resource, political and symbolic. If applied to UFV and the project, these will indicate where support and resistance can be found.

Structural frame. UFV has campuses in Abbotsford, Chilliwack, Mission and Hope (Maclean’s, n.d.). Its Health Sciences programs, including nursing, health care assistant and dental assisting, are based mainly at the Chilliwack campus, while kinesiology courses are offered at both the Chilliwack and Abbotsford campuses (Russell, 2012; UFV, 2021). The project therefore establishes a formal Academy within the Faculty of Health Sciences, with a written agreement covering space, faculty and budget.

Human resource frame. Individuals require purposeful work and development. The Academy provides students with clinical placements and surge-readiness credentials, and gives faculty research opportunities. This makes the project a benefit to UFV, not a burden.

Political frame. Funding and space are contested between departments. Although Fraser Health hosts the centre, the municipalities and the province might have different ideas about how it should be governed. Each is given a seat on the steering committee, and the committee publishes its decisions so that conflicts are handled openly.

Symbolic frame. Symbols create meaning. Naming the Academy and the centre after the community, holding a public opening and publicly recognizing reserve corps members show that this is a long-term partnership and not a one-off project.

7. The Champion

Project champions are high-level individuals who advocate for the project, defend it from interference and clear the way for it (Schwalbe, 2019). FV-POC needs two. The executive champion is the senior federal official who signs the funding agreement and chairs the steering committee. The regional champion is a senior Fraser Health executive, a highly trusted health leader in the Fraser Valley who can convene Fraser Health, the municipalities and the First Nations at one table and serve as the project’s “face” in the community. Because Fraser Health hosts the centre, the Director reports to the regional champion and to the steering committee. The project manager, Yatharth Sharma, is responsible to both champions.

9. Project Life Cycle Model

According to Schwalbe (2019), there are two life cycle types: predictive (waterfall), in which the scope of the product is determined early in the life cycle, and adaptive (spiral or agile), in which the product evolves through repeated cycles. FV-POC uses a hybrid approach. Construction, procurement and equipment purchases follow a waterfall approach because of fixed designs, building codes and contracts, and because changes late in construction are expensive. The data platform, dashboard and alert service are developed incrementally and released in iterations every couple of months, because users’ requirements are not fully understood until they have tried the tools themselves. The spiral idea of checking risk at the end of each cycle is applied across the whole project through the six go / no-go gates in the milestone table.

11. Pre-Initiation Tasks

Before the project officially launches, several questions need to be asked and answered to decide whether it is advisable to proceed (Schwalbe, 2019). For FV-POC they are: (a) confirm the funding source and conditions in writing; (b) complete a needs assessment using the evidence in Section A; (c) compare alternatives (for example, a new hospital or an app alone) and record the reasons for choosing this option; (d) estimate costs and benefits at a high level (Section 14); (e) identify the executive and regional champions; (f) hold early discussions with Fraser Health, the municipalities and First Nations to test support; (g) assign a pre-initiation lead and a small team; and (h) request formal approval to proceed to the charter. The most significant pre-initiation document is this proposal.

14. Return on Investment and Payback Analysis

Return on investment (ROI) and payback can be considered as comparing the benefits with cost (Schwalbe, 2019). Following the example of Fan et al. (2018), the benefit of preparedness is a loss that did not occur, so the analysis is based on an expected-loss approach, which is the probability of pandemic times its cost.

Approximate global pandemic losses suggested by Fan et al. (2018) are around 0.6 per cent of the annual income. If that’s applied to the assumed regional economy of $100 billion (which should be checked with the data from BC Stats and/or Statistics Canada) then the loss is estimated at $600 million annually. This risk cannot be completely removed as part of the project and some scenarios are presented below with different percentages of the loss avoided.

Expected loss avoided, %Yearly benefitSimple payback (years)10-year undiscounted net ROI (benefit / cost)
10% (cautious)$60M16.7 years-40%
25% (base case)$150M6.7 years+50%
40% (strong)$240M4.2 years+140%

The base case calculation is: $600M × 25% = $150M per year; $1,000M ÷ $150M = 6.7 years; and over ten years, ($1,500M − $1,000M) ÷ $1,000M = 50 per cent. The findings do not account for the value of lives saved and illnesses averted, which pandemic studies indicate is a large percentage of the pandemic losses (Fan et al., 2018). They also don’t cover benefits due to floods or other emergencies. Given the same $100 billion assumption, then having a similar economic impact to the 2020 drop on production (5.3 per cent; Statistics Canada, 2021) would be estimated at just over $5.3 billion in one year. This analysis should be repeated in future versions, using more scenarios and benefit discounted to present value. The comparison also uses only the $1 billion build cost and leaves out operating costs after the sustainment reserve ends in 2033, which would lengthen the payback period.

15. Project Quality Management

Quality management ensures that the project is delivered in line with its purpose. It consists of three components: planning quality, assuring quality and controlling quality (PMI, 2021; Schwalbe, 2019). The quality plan sets measurable standards for FV-POC, such as wastewater results sent within 48 hours, 90 days of supplies on hand, alerts sent in the top ten local languages and, most importantly, no privacy violations.

Quality planning sets the standards, drawing on regulations, health authority policies and the lessons learned from the audits mentioned in Section A. Quality assurance is carried out through independent audits at each gate and peer review of designs. Quality control uses inspections, test results and defect logs, with checks after each deliverable. All requests for changes are written down, assessed for cost, schedule and risk by a change-control board, and then either approved by the steering committee or rejected. This helps prevent scope from creeping in unnoticed.

17. Testing and Cost of Quality

Testing is done to discover problems before they affect the public. Unit testing, integration testing, system testing and user acceptance testing (Schwalbe, 2019) are used for the technology components. Airflow, back-up power and flood-resistance tests are conducted on the buildings, and the buildings are commissioned. The laboratory takes part in proficiency testing, and real community volunteers are used to test the alert system. The three full-scale exercises are intended to test everything together, and each exercise ends with a written report and a correction plan.

According to Schwalbe (2019), the cost of quality is the total of the conformance costs (prevention and appraisal) and the nonconformance costs (internal and external failure). Crosby (1979) explained that quality is free, because it costs less to prevent errors than to fix them. In this project, prevention covers training and design reviews, and appraisal covers inspections, audits and exercises ($9 million in the budget) as well as the independent audit. A failed response during a real pandemic would be the most costly outcome of all.

19. Motivating People

Herzberg (1968) argued that pay, conditions and job security prevent dissatisfaction, but achievement, recognition, meaningful work and growth are what motivate people to work. FV-POC applies this in four ways. First, it offers competitive wages and benefits and safe working conditions to avoid dissatisfaction. Second, it gives staff a clear and visible mission: protecting the most vulnerable older people and the workers who were most affected by the previous pandemic. Third, it provides opportunities for development, such as UFV credentials, promotion paths and travel to conferences. Fourth, it openly recognizes achievement through team awards after exercises and an annual report on achievements. Reservists are recognized, trained and given a community stipend. Team morale is monitored regularly and reported to the steering committee, because low morale is an early sign of trouble.

24. Handling Stakeholders

Stakeholder management involves classifying stakeholders by their influence and interest, and then choosing an approach for each group (PMI, 2021). High-influence, high-interest groups, such as the sponsor, Fraser Health, the municipalities and First Nations, are managed closely and are represented on the steering committee or a joint working group. High-interest, low-influence groups, such as seniors, families and farmworkers, are informed and consulted through advisory groups and community visits, because they were the most affected by the pandemic. The privacy commissioner and other groups with high influence but low day-to-day interest are kept satisfied with regular briefings and early access to privacy impact assessments. Vendors are monitored through contract management.

A stakeholder survey is conducted twice a year, and a stakeholder register is used to track engagement. If support falls, the project manager and the champion meet with the affected group within two weeks to develop a recovery plan. Problems must not be ignored, since known problems that are not corrected are the ones that become failures (OAG, 2021).

25. Closing Process

The closing process group is the final process group. It involves finalizing all project activities and formally ending the project (PMI, 2021). FV-POC will begin closing in the fourth quarter of 2029 and will finish on December 31, 2029.

  • Final acceptance: the sponsor and the operating authority (Fraser Health) sign off on each of the nine deliverables against the acceptance tests.
  • Administrative closure: a final cost report is produced, all budget lines are reconciled, and any unused contingency is returned to the sponsor or added to the sustainment reserve, as agreed.
  • Contract closure: all vendor contracts are finalized, warranties are registered, final payments are made and disputes are settled.
  • Handover: all assets, manuals, passwords and the risk register pass to the control of the Director and permanent staff, and the sustainment reserve begins funding operations.
  • Lessons learned: a facilitated session with the team, partners and community members is held, and the results are written up and shared publicly.
  • People: project staff are thanked publicly and helped to move to permanent roles or other work.
  • Archive and review: records are stored securely, and a post-project review is scheduled for 12 months later to measure whether the benefits in Section 14 are on track.
